A Note on Why

The question I get asked most

"Psychology and social entrepreneurship — so what does that actually make you?" It's the question almost everyone asks when they see my background, and honestly, there isn't a tidy job title for the answer.

Here's the closest I've got: I want to be a changemaker — someone who uses research, design and community-building to make the world a little better, in whatever form that takes. Mental health is where that drive feels most personal to me, but the same lens applies anywhere people are navigating systems that weren't quite built with them in mind — a nightlife area that feels unsafe after dark, a community event that needs to outlive its own funding, a product that gets the data right but misses how people actually feel.

My interdisciplinary background isn't a detour between two unrelated degrees — it's the toolkit. Psychology gives me the why: an understanding of how people think, feel and behave. Social entrepreneurship gives me the how: the tools to build something — a programme, a product, a piece of research — around that understanding, and to make it last.

MSc Dissertation: Decentering, Mindfulness & Rumination

Sample
283 UK adults, 18–30
Method
Mediation analysis
Focus
Mindfulness · Decentering · Rumination
Key finding: Decentering — not awareness alone — explains why mindfulness relates to lower rumination.
+ Tap to read the full study

My dissertation asked a question I found both scientifically and personally compelling: how does mindfulness relate to repetitive negative thinking — and what process might explain that link? I focused on three constructs: mindfulness, decentering (the ability to step back from thoughts and view them as temporary mental events rather than facts), and rumination.

Using a cross-sectional online survey distributed through Qualtrics and Prolific, I recruited 310 participants and, after data cleaning, worked with a final sample of 0 UK-based young adults aged 18–30. Each completed validated measures of mindfulness, decentering and rumination, which I analysed using correlation and mediation models.

Mindfulness was positively associated with decentering, and decentering was associated with lower rumination — though mindfulness itself showed a small positive association with rumination in this sample. The mediation analysis pointed to decentering as the key mechanism: mindfulness related to lower rumination specifically through the ability to step back from thoughts, not simply through noticing them more.

The project reinforced something I keep returning to in my work: awareness alone isn't always enough — how we relate to our thoughts may matter just as much as whether we notice them. It sharpened my skills in research design, ethics, survey development, participant recruitment, data cleaning, statistical analysis and communicating findings clearly. I'm grateful to the University of Exeter Psychology faculty for funding the participant recruitment that made this research possible.

Decentering carries mindfulness's effect: more mindfulness related to more decentering (path a), which in turn related to lower rumination (path b).

LIMELIGHT: Designing for Perceived Safety in Bangkok

Method
Mixed-methods UX research
Usability
86% SUS · 7s alert
Tools
Adobe XD · Miro
Key finding: Tourists' fear came from perceived threat, not actual crime — reframing the brief from policing to companionship.
+ Tap to read the full case study

As part of a design-thinking research project, my partner and I set out to understand why tourists felt unsafe walking around Soi Nana, a nightlife district in Bangkok — even though it has a reputation as a vibrant, welcoming area. We combined seven in-depth interviews, four ad-hoc street interviews, direct observation across two field visits, and official crime-report data that we requested directly from the local police station.

The data told two different stories: residents felt safe, citing close-knit community ties, while every tourist we spoke to felt unsafe at night — despite crime statistics showing the area was, in reality, low-risk. That contradiction became the heart of the project. We reframed the brief from "reduce crime" to "reduce perceived threat" — a problem about feelings and environment, not actual danger.

From ten brainstormed concepts — including a police statue, a 10pm curfew and a "walking game" — we ran a "worst ideas" exercise to extract lessons, then used a complexity/impact matrix to land on LIMELIGHT: a website that turns nearby users into an informal safety network, with an emergency alert button, live status updates, and route guidance to the nearest open cafe or bar.

I designed the interface in Adobe XD, from low-fidelity wireframes through to a high-fidelity UI with a deliberately chosen colour palette, then ran usability testing with five participants. Beyond the standard System Usability Scale, I added a twist suited to an emergency product: timing how quickly users could activate the alert under simulated urgency.

Kaleidoscope: Auditing a Live Crime-Prevention System

Client
South West Business Crime Centre
Method
Independent structured audit
Delivered
Jun 2026
Key finding: A data retention gap was quietly deleting real casework history instead of archiving it.
+ Tap to read the full audit

South West Business Crime Centre is an independent, 20+ year business crime reduction consultancy that supports Business Improvement Districts (BIDs) across the UK, including Bristol, Bath, Salisbury, and Weston-super-Mare, in running shared crime prevention partnerships. At the centre of that work is Kaleidoscope, a criminal profile tracking system used by analysts to record and manage profiles of individuals flagged across member businesses, exactly the kind of sensitive, high-stakes tool where usability and data integrity genuinely matter to real people's outcomes.

I approached the founder directly and secured an independent structured audit of the system, reviewing interaction design, information architecture, and data-handling practices across the platform, then wrote up my findings as a formal report rather than informal feedback, prioritised by risk and paired with implementation-ready recommendations.

The audit surfaced three distinct issues. A critical interaction bug, caused by a layout overlap and conflicting z-index, was blocking the profile "Close" button entirely, trapping analysts inside a modal view with no way out short of a full page reload. A weak visual hierarchy on individual profile pages was slowing down analysts during time-sensitive casework, which I addressed with a proposed redesign using consistent grid alignment and collapsible or tabbed sections. But the most consequential finding was a data retention gap: profiles belonging to incarcerated individuals were being permanently deleted from the system rather than archived, silently erasing historical network and case data that could matter for future casework or audits.

I delivered the report directly to the founder, who relayed it to the development team for implementation. It's a small project on paper, one audit, one client, but it's the piece of work I'd point to first if asked what "research that actually changes something" looks like: the data retention finding wasn't a UI polish note, it was a quiet risk to real institutional memory that nobody had flagged yet.

Nang Loeng Immersive Exhibition: Building for Handover

Visitors
450+
Team
30 people · 5 depts
Reach
230.6K (+848%)
Key lesson: Authority doesn't make a leader — legitimacy built through relationships is what makes cross-functional collaboration work.
+ Tap to read the full story

As Project Manager, I led a 30-person team across five departments — Marketing, Media Production, Design, Logistics and Business Partnerships — through an 8-phase, 7-month journey (research, recruitment, team-building, planning, promotion, design, pre-execution and execution) to deliver a 7-day, 11-programme exhibition celebrating Nang Loeng, one of Bangkok's oldest communities, which drew over 450 visitors.

Rather than relying on existing activities, we designed and launched four new revenue-generating experiences from scratch: a food-tasting trail, a food-hunting "treasure hunt" across 17 partner cafes and restaurants plus 2 hostels, guided heritage walks, and craft workshops. Together they generated roughly 29,250 THB (approx. £676) within the first week, reinvested directly into the community — with the food-hunting activity alone reaching 458 redemptions. Alongside the research and delivery work, I also led the negotiations that secured a business partnership investment into the project.

On the media side, our social following grew to 4,186, with 230.6K reach (+848%) and 88.2K engagement (+1234%) over 90 days — one Reel alone reached 86K accounts with 94K plays. We contacted 63 media outlets and secured coverage from 18, including the Bangkok Post, Time Out, The Standard and Urban Creature.

The part I'm proudest of is what happened after the exhibition ended: rather than just running an event, we designed for handover. We transferred the social media accounts, contact databases, partnership frameworks and ran capacity-building sessions so the Nang Loeng community could continue independently. Along the way, I learned that authority doesn't guarantee leadership — legitimacy, built through relationships and shared goals, is what actually makes cross-functional collaboration work, especially when navigating the internal politics of multiple community leaders who didn't always see eye to eye.
